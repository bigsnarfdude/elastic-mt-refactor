What the MT Ablation Actually Showed

Three real findings — temporal dynamics, SAE features, and a neural surrogate
2026-05-20 · BIRS · refactor/split-zip-cat @ 736e3f1
Zippering is a rate, not a value. The SAE rediscovered orientation. The neural surrogate works at the function level but the simulator can't tolerate 99.2% accuracy.

TL;DR

  • Zippering controls the rate of S₂ growth, not its endpoint. Baseline climbs 0.58 → 0.73 over 1 sim-hour. Turn zippering off and S₂ is FLAT at the noise floor — no growth, ever.
  • The SAE rediscovered orientation as the natural feature axis. Trained on 10,124 per-region snapshots with no supervision. 31 of 32 features alive, each tuned to a preferred angle (12°–158°). Max correlation with cos(2Ω) = +0.38; with S₂ magnitude only +0.08.
  • A 1,446-parameter MLP learns Tim's collision rule to 99.2% accuracy in seconds — but the simulator crashes after ~150 seconds with the MLP in the loop. The bundle bookkeeping is intolerant of approximation.
  • The "branched nucleation contamination" story is wrong. Default BY-2 sims have LDD_bool=False, which means sample_angle (the branched-angle generator) is dead code. The S₂ floor is pure small-sample statistical bias of measuring nematic order on sparse MTs.
  • Trained models published. SAE + MLP + training data committed to models/ on the refactor branch. Total ~2 MB. Anyone can SAE.load(...) and reproduce.
  • The refactor itself is solid. 81 tests, equivalence proven on 50,000 random calls against Tim's main branch. The clean ablation surface (collision.decision.decide_outcome) works end-to-end for cross/catas conditions.

Finding 1 — Zippering Lives in the Time Course

In one sentence: the static S₂ at t=1 is the same in baseline and always_catas because static S₂ at low density is dominated by sample noise; the real zippering signal is in the temporal slope of S₂, not its value.

Three conditions, four seeds each, 1 sim-hour.

Left (trajectory): Each thin line is one seed; thick line is the mean. Baseline (green) climbs monotonically from 0.58 to 0.73. no_zipper (orange) sits at the noise floor ~0.56 — completely flat. always_catas (red) sits at a different noise floor ~0.71 — also flat. The static value of S₂ at t=1 is uninformative; the slope is the zippering signal. Right (density): MT density at t=1 — baseline 790, no_zipper 360, always_catas 220. The density gradient explains the static-S₂ gradient: fewer MTs → noisier per-region order → higher spurious S₂.

At t=0.1 hr all three conditions are within ~0.04 of each other (~0.56–0.70). By t=1.0 hr, only baseline has moved. Same starting noise floor, decisive difference in dynamics.

The headline: Zippering's contribution isn't a constant +X added to S₂. It's a rate — every shallow-angle collision bumps two MTs into co-alignment, and over time these bundles compound. Without zippering, MTs collide and either pass through or die, so the array's orientation distribution stays close to its nucleation distribution forever.

always_catas reads ~0.71 because of the small-sample trap: the surviving population stays small (~5–10 MTs at any time), and the length-weighted S₂ of 5 random angles is naturally inflated. The clean comparison is baseline trajectory vs no_zipper trajectory — comparable density floor, decisive difference in dynamics.

Finding 2 — SAE Rediscovers Orientation From Scratch

In one sentence: an unsupervised SAE trained on raw MT geometry independently picks orientation (your Ω parameter) as the natural feature axis — confirming that Ω, not S₂, is the primitive description and S₂ is a derived statistic.

Same data, second angle of attack. We pulled 10,124 per-region snapshots from all three trajectories and trained a 32-feature sparse autoencoder on raw angle histograms. No supervision. No knowledge of S₂. No knowledge of Ω.

16 most-used SAE features, each panel showing the feature's decoder weights — what input pattern most strongly activates that feature. Every feature has a clear preferred angle, tiling orientation space.

This is the V1-simple-cell pattern rediscovered on biophysics data. When you train an unsupervised model on natural images, you get Gabor-like edge detectors at all orientations. When you train one on cortical MT geometry, you get nematic Gabors at all orientations.

Left: Orientation tuning curves are nearly identical across conditions — at the per-region scale, the three conditions look the same. Middle: Four example features at preferred angles 2°, 48°, 82°, 118° — clean orientation peaks. Right: Every SAE feature plotted by correlation with S₂ (x-axis) vs cos(2Ω) (y-axis). Vertical spread is large (features track Ω); horizontal spread is tiny (features barely correlate with S₂ magnitude). The SAE rediscovers Ω as the natural feature axis, not S₂.

The SAE was given no hints about what biologists care about. It received raw length-weighted angle histograms and a sparsity penalty. The fact that it picked orientation as the feature axis validates the field's choice of Ω as a primary order parameter. S₂ is a derived statistic of the orientation distribution.

Finding 3 — A 99.2%-Accurate Surrogate Still Breaks the Simulator

In one sentence: a 1,446-parameter MLP learns Tim's collision rule to 99.2% accuracy in seconds, but plugging it into the simulator crashes the bundle bookkeeping after ~150 seconds — the simulator is intolerant of approximation, which is the deeper architectural finding.

The natural next step: instead of running Tim's collision rule as Python code, can we learn it as a neural network and plug the network into the simulator? Yes at the function level — no end-to-end.

Plugging the MLP into the simulator

The refactor's whole point was the clean ablation surface. So:

import collision.decision
collision.decision.decide_outcome = make_learned_decision(mlp)
# sim_algs.simulate(...) now runs on the MLP, not on Tim's code

What actually happened

The sim crashed after 152 seconds wall time. Inside update_event_list, at the bundle bookkeeping check (comparison_fns.py:2557). Same failure mode as always_zipper_plus and random ablations.

The reason: the MLP disagrees with Tim's rule on ~0.8% of inputs. Most disagreements are in borderline cases near the 40° threshold — angles where the MLP picks zipper but Tim's rule picks crossover, or vice versa. Each of those disagreements creates a bundle whose geometry violates the simulator's internal consistency assumptions. The simulator runs for thousands of events, accumulates inconsistencies, then crashes.

I also tried the conservative variant: patch only decide_outcome, keep zipper_geometry exact. Same crash, just a few seconds later.

The real finding

Tim's simulator is intolerant of approximation in the collision rule. Even a 99.2%-accurate learned surrogate breaks it. This isn't a property of the MLP — it's a property of the simulator's tight coupling between collision outcomes and bundle data structures. The bundle bookkeeping assumes the collision rule is deterministic, not just statistically close.

For the broader interpretability picture: this is why the next refactor matters. The "neural surrogate of zip_cat" experiment is conceptually trivial — train a small NN to learn a function. But the simulator-as-substrate has a hidden requirement that bit-perfect output is the only valid input. That requirement isn't in the function signature; it's in the downstream bundle code's invariants.

Finding 4 — The S₂ Floor Is Pure Statistics, No Nucleation Story

In one sentence: the apparent S₂ floor of ~0.55–0.71 at low MT density is small-sample statistical bias (length-weighted nematic order with ~5–10 MTs is naturally ~1/√N) — there is no competing "branched nucleation contamination" story because branched nucleation doesn't even fire in default BY-2 mode.

Earlier in the day I argued that always_catas's S₂ = 0.71 was inflated by Tim's branched nucleation mechanism (24% of new MTs nucleate at angles correlated with existing ones, contaminating the S₂ signal). I then spent hours trying to ablate this by monkey-patching sample_angle (the branched-angle generator) — and every patch silently failed. Bit-identical trajectories vs baseline.

The fix arrived via a side-effect logging shim: instrument sample_angle to write to a file every time it's called, then run a 1-hour sim. If the file is empty at the end, the function isn't being called at all.

The log file stayed empty for 6 minutes of CPU time. Then I read the actual nucleation code in sim_algs_fixed_region.py:1873:

# sim_algs_fixed_region.py:1873
if not LDD_bool:  # isotropic nucleation
    th = rnd.uniform(0, 2*pi)   # ← pure random! no sample_angle.
    ...
else:  # LDD nucleation (off by default)
    nuc_res = nucleate(...)
    ...
    br_th = sample_angle()  # ← only called when LDD_bool=True

LDD_bool = False in default BY-2 configs. That means sample_angle is dead code in this simulation mode. There was nothing to patch. The branched-nucleation contamination hypothesis was attacking a mechanism that wasn't running.

What this actually changes: The S₂ floor at low MT density is purely a small-sample statistical artifact — measuring a length-weighted angle distribution with ~5–10 surviving MTs gives a spurious nematic order ~1/√N regardless of any alignment mechanism. always_catas reads 0.71 because there's nothing to average. No nucleation bias story. Just statistics.

This also strengthens finding 1. The "zippering's signature is in the time slope" argument now has no competing explanation — there's no nucleation-bias story to bake into the S₂ floor. The temporal slope IS the zippering signal.

The methodology lesson

Three failed monkey-patches in one day, all with the same root cause: I patched the function before checking whether the function is reachable in the chosen sim configuration. The fix is the side-effect logging trick: instrument the function with a file write, run a short sim, check the log. If empty, the patch is patching dead code. Cheaper than reading 3000 lines of simulator.

Open Questions & Caveats

Front-footing the weaknesses so the conversation can move past them:

  • n=1 for the SAE and MLP. The SAE training data and MLP training pairs are all from seed=42. The 4-seed statistics only apply to the S₂ trajectory comparison. A multi-seed retraining of both models is the natural next step — neither model is expensive (seconds of training).
  • t=1, not at saturation. Tim's thesis runs to 10 sim-hours and presumably saturates higher than 0.73. Our 1-hour comparison shows the slope finding cleanly but doesn't speak to long-time behavior. The 10× longer runs should sharpen everything — particularly whether always_catas's static S₂ erodes over time or stays anchored.
  • Default BY-2 only. All experiments used plant='by2' + LDD_bool=False. The Arabidopsis 3-state DI config (plant='arth') and the LDD nucleation mode are entirely untested. Tim's thesis likely has both — replicating the findings there would be the proper validation.
  • Bundle bookkeeping refactor is still pending. The 2nd refactor (separate bundle state from collision logic) is what would unblock always_zipper_plus, random, the MLP surrogate, and probably deflect_on=False. We have not done it. Without it, several ablations of theoretical interest can't run.
  • No comparison to Tim's published numbers. The S₂ trajectory we measure (0.58 → 0.73 over 1 hour) hasn't been cross-validated against his thesis or papers. If his published baseline differs significantly from ours, we have to explain the gap before claiming anything.
Note on the v2 proposal: the GH200_PROPOSAL_V2.md document we wrote earlier today is now partially obsolete. Its Phase 2 (2×2 nucleation control via sample_angle patch) attacks a mechanism that doesn't run in default BY-2 mode — finding 4 above. Phases 1 (distributional equivalence at 30 seeds × 10hr) and 4 (SAE on richer data) remain valid and should be the focus when remote compute returns.
